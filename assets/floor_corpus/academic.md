The proliferation of productivity software presents a paradox that merits closer examination; namely, that the adoption of tools designed to reduce cognitive burden frequently increases it. Empirical observation suggests that the maintenance costs associated with organizational systems — taxonomies, hierarchies, and tagging schemes — are systematically underestimated by their users. This underestimation is not incidental; rather, it reflects a broader tendency to conflate the appearance of order with the substance of progress.

Consider the case of hierarchical file organization. The user who constructs an elaborate folder structure incurs an immediate cost in time and attention; moreover, the structure imposes ongoing costs, as each new item demands classification. The alternative — reliance upon search and associative linking — externalizes this burden to the retrieval stage, where modern indexing renders it negligible. It follows that the rational strategy, for most users, is the abandonment of prospective organization in favor of retrospective retrieval; the persistence of the former practice must therefore be explained by reference to its psychological, rather than its instrumental, functions.
